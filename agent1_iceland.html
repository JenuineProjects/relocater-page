1. Job Market & Career Prospects

Pin1 — HR Professional (BAH Hons HRM + MSc AI for Business) → DEI Ambitions

The good news: Iceland has a tight labour market. Unemployment has hovered around 3–5% for years. HR professionals are needed, particularly in larger companies (banks like Landsbankinn, Íslandsbanki; tech companies like Marel, Controlant, Aha; airlines like Icelandair and PLAY). Your MSc in AI for Business is a genuine differentiator — Icelandic companies are actively digitising, and HR tech / people analytics is an area where demand outstrips local supply.

DEI specifically — let me be blunt:

Iceland has a strong track record on gender equality — we've topped the World Economic Forum's Global Gender Gap Index for over a decade. But "DEI" as a formalised professional discipline, the way it exists in the US, UK, or increasingly in Ireland? It barely exists here as a standalone career path.

Here's why:

  • Iceland is extremely homogeneous. The population was ~95% ethnically Icelandic until relatively recently. Immigration has increased significantly (foreign-born residents now make up ~15–18% of the population), but the institutional response is still catching up.
  • Gender equality work is well-established — the Directorate of Equality (Jafnréttisstofa) and related bodies exist, and companies above a certain size are legally required to obtain equal pay certification (Jafnlaunavottun). This is a genuine area where HR professionals with DEI knowledge can add value.
  • But dedicated DEI roles are almost non-existent. You won't find "Head of DEI" positions at Icelandic companies. Equality and inclusion work is typically folded into broader HR roles, or handled by one person at a government agency. There might be 1–3 such specialist positions open in the entire country at any given time — often at the University of Iceland, the City of Reykjavík, or a government directorate.
  • Where your angle could work: Positioning yourself as an HR professional who brings DEI expertise as part of a broader HR skill set — particularly combined with your AI/analytics background — is far more marketable than pitching yourself as a pure DEI specialist. Icelandic companies dealing with an increasingly diverse workforce (fish processing, tourism, construction — sectors with high immigrant labour) need HR people who understand inclusion, even if they don't call it "DEI."
  • The multicultural/integration sector is small but growing. Organisations like the Multicultural and Information Centre (Fjölmenningarsetur), the Directorate of Immigration (Útlendingastofnun), and various municipality integration programmes occasionally hire people with relevant backgrounds. But again — Icelandic language is typically required.

The hard truth remains:

  • Almost all HR roles require fluent Icelandic. You'll be writing employment contracts, handling union negotiations (unions are enormously powerful here — ~90% membership rate), and navigating Icelandic labour law. Without Icelandic, you're limited to international companies or English-speaking tech startups.
  • The AI-for-business angle could open doors in consulting or tech, but these roles are few. You might find 2–5 relevant openings at any given time in the entire country.
  • Salaries for HR professionals range roughly ISK 550,000–850,000/month (approx. €3,600–€5,600) depending on seniority. Senior HRBP or HR Director roles can reach ISK 1,000,000+, but those require Icelandic fluency and local network.
  • DEI-adjacent roles in the public/non-profit sector pay at the lower end: ISK 500,000–700,000/month (€3,300–€4,600).
  • Key job boards: Alfred.is, Tvinna.is, LinkedIn (limited but growing), Starfatorg.is (public sector).

Pin2 — Marketing, Communications & Digital Content (BAH Media Production & Digital Arts) + Human Geography Interest

The broader marketing/comms picture:

Expanding beyond NGOs is wise, because the Icelandic marketing and communications sector — while small — is more active than you might expect.

Private sector opportunities:

  • Tourism industry: Even post-pandemic, tourism is one of Iceland's economic pillars. Hotels, tour operators, travel platforms (Guide to Iceland, Bókun, Hey Iceland) regularly need marketing and content people. This is probably the sector most willing to hire in English, because the audience is international.
  • Tech companies: Reykjavík's tech scene (CCP Games, Controlant, Marel, Treble Technologies, various startups) needs digital marketing, content creators, and social media managers. Some operate primarily in English.
  • Creative agencies: There are several agencies in Reykjavík — Brandenburg, Íslenska, Jónsson & Le'macks, Hvíta húsið — that handle marketing for major Icelandic brands. These are competitive to get into and almost always require Icelandic, but freelance/contract work is possible.
  • Retail & consumer brands: Icelandic brands with international ambitions (66°North, Össur, Blue Lagoon) sometimes need English-language content creators and digital marketers.

NGO sector (still relevant):

  • Iceland has organisations like the Icelandic Red Cross (Rauði krossinn), Amnesty International Iceland, the Icelandic Human Rights Centre, Samtökin '78 (the national LGBTQ+ org), and various environmental groups. They need marketing/comms people but the sector is tiny — maybe 1–2 relevant roles per year across all NGOs combined.

Public sector:

  • The City of Reykjavík, government ministries, and public institutions (national museums, Harpa, the University of Iceland) occasionally hire communications officers. These roles almost always require Icelandic.

Freelance/self-employment:

  • This is genuinely worth considering. Iceland has a straightforward system for registering as a sole trader (einstaklingur í atvinnurekstri). Pin2's media production and digital arts skills are highly freelanceable — video production, content creation, social media management, graphic design. The creative community in Reykjavík is small and collaborative; once you're known, word-of-mouth carries far in a country this size.

Human Geography:

  • The University of Iceland (Háskóli Íslands) has a Faculty of Life and Environmental Sciences with geography and tourism studies programmes. There's also the University of Akureyri. Iceland is, frankly, a fascinating place to study human geography — settlement patterns, resource management, climate adaptation, volcanic hazard planning, rural depopulation, Arctic geopolitics. Academic positions and research roles exist but are very limited and highly competitive. This might be better as a postgraduate study path than an immediate career — and tuition at the University of Iceland is essentially free (just a registration fee of ~ISK 75,000/€500 per year) for EEA citizens.
  • Applied human geography work exists in municipal planning, environmental impact assessment, and tourism management, but these roles require Icelandic.

Salary expectations for marketing/comms roles:

  • Private sector: ISK 500,000–800,000/month (€3,300–€5,300) depending on seniority and company size
  • NGO sector: ISK 450,000–650,000/month (€3,000–€4,300)
  • Freelance: highly variable — could be lucrative in tourism season, lean in winter

The hard truths remain:

  • The Icelandic language barrier limits you severely in most sectors. Tourism and tech are the exceptions.
  • We're talking about a country of ~385,000 people. The entire marketing industry is smaller than a mid-size Dublin agency.
  • Pin2's digital arts / media production skills are broadly marketable, but building a client base takes time.

Overall Job Market Verdict

You're both employable in theory, but the language barrier is the single biggest obstacle. Plan for 6–12 months of limited employment options while you learn Icelandic. Having savings to cover this period is essential. As EEA citizens (Ireland), you have full work rights — no visa needed.

Pin1's best short-term play is HR roles at international or tech companies, weaving in DEI expertise as a value-add rather than leading with it. Pin2's best short-term play is tourism-sector marketing or freelance content creation while building language skills for the broader market.

Housing Crisis

I won't sugarcoat this: Iceland has a severe housing shortage, especially in Reykjavík. The rental market is brutal.

  • There is no real regulated private rental market like in many European countries. Most rentals are informal — individuals renting out apartments through Facebook groups (Leiga á Íslandi, Leiguskjól) or Mbl.is classifieds.
  • Expect to compete with dozens of applicants for every decent listing.
  • Landlords often prefer Icelanders or people with existing Icelandic references.
  • A deposit of 1–3 months' rent is standard.
  • Pet-friendly rentals are harder to find. Mention the cats upfront — some landlords won't accept pets. This narrows your already narrow options further.
  • Buying property: Foreigners from EEA countries can buy property. Prices in Reykjavík for a 60–80 m² apartment range from ISK 45–75 million (€300,000–€500,000). You'll need a kennitala (national ID number) and typically a 20–25% down payment. Mortgage rates have been volatile — currently around 7–9% for indexed loans.

A note on food & alcohol

Groceries are expensive. A litre of milk is ~€1.80, a block of cheese ~€7–10. Alcohol is state-controlled and sold only at Vínbúðin (the state monopoly). A bottle of wine starts at ~€12–15 and a pint at a bar is €10–14. If you enjoy a drink, budget accordingly or learn to love the duty-free shop at Keflavík Airport.

3. Vegetarian Life in Iceland

The honest picture: it's gotten much better, but it's still not easy.

Iceland's food culture is historically built on fish, lamb, and dairy. Vegetarianism was genuinely rare here until about 10–15 years ago. The shift has been real but incomplete.

Supermarket Shopping

What you'll find easily:

  • Basic vegetables, fruits, and salad ingredients are available year-round at Bónus, Krónan, Hagkaup, and Nettó (the main supermarket chains). However, variety is more limited than what you're used to in Ireland, and quality of fresh produce can be hit-or-miss — almost everything is imported and has travelled a long way.
  • Icelandic greenhouse produce is a bright spot. Thanks to geothermal energy, Iceland grows tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, herbs, and even some leafy greens in heated greenhouses (Friðheimar is the famous one). These are fresh, local, and available year-round. Look for the "Íslenski garðurinn" branding.
  • Dairy is excellent and relatively affordable — Icelandic skyr is world-class, and there's good cheese, milk, and butter.
  • Eggs are readily available and of good quality.
  • Rice, pasta, bread, beans, lentils, and basic staples — no problem.

What's harder:

  • Specialty vegetarian/vegan products (plant-based meats, tofu, tempeh, specific meat substitutes): Available but limited and expensive. Hagkaup has the best selection. You'll find Oatly, some Beyond/plant-based products, and tofu, but the range is a fraction of what a large Tesco or Dunnes in Ireland carries. Expect to pay a 30–50% premium over Irish prices for these items.
  • Variety of fresh vegetables: Don't expect the same range as an Irish or continental European supermarket. Things like aubergine, courgette, fresh herbs beyond basics, and Asian vegetables are available but can be expensive and not always fresh.
  • Beans and pulses: Dried beans and lentils are available but the selection is limited. Canned beans are easier to find. If you cook a lot of Indian or Middle Eastern food (common for vegetarians), stock up on spices — the selection here is basic compared to what you'd find in Irish shops with immigrant communities.

Eating Out

The good news: Reykjavík's restaurant scene has transformed over the past decade. Vegetarian options are now standard at most restaurants in central Reykjavík. You won't struggle to find something to eat.

Specifically:

  • Dedicated vegetarian/vegan restaurants: A handful exist in Reykjavík. Kaffi Vínyl (vegan), Jómfrúin (has good vegetarian options), and a few others rotate in and out. The scene is small but present.
  • Most mainstream restaurants in Reykjavík now offer at least 1–2 vegetarian mains. Icelandic "new Nordic" restaurants often have creative vegetable dishes. Pizza, pasta, and Asian restaurants are reliable standbys.
  • Cafés: Reykjavík's café culture is strong, and most cafés offer vegetarian soups, sandwiches, and salads.
  • Fast food: Hlöllabátar (Iceland's beloved sub sandwich chain) has veggie options. Noodle Station does a solid vegetable soup. Various burger places now offer plant-based patties.

The challenges:

  • Outside Reykjavík: This is where it gets tough. In smaller towns, gas station restaurants, and rural areas, vegetarian options can be limited to chips, bread, or a sad salad. On road trips, pack food. Seriously.
  • Traditional Icelandic food is almost entirely animal-based. Þorrablót (the midwinter feast) is literally a celebration of fermented shark, dried fish, blood pudding, and ram testicles. You will be sitting those out.
  • Price: Eating out is expensive for everyone, but vegetarian dishes are usually (mercifully) among the cheaper options on menus, since you're not paying for Icelandic lamb or fresh-caught fish. A vegetarian main at a decent restaurant runs €18–30; at a casual place, €12–20.
  • Social dining: Icelanders are increasingly aware of vegetarianism, and you won't face hostility or confusion. But you may get the occasional "but you eat fish, right?" — the concept of full vegetarianism is still newer here than in Ireland or the UK.

Home Cooking Reality

If you cook most of your meals at home — which you'll probably need to for budget reasons anyway — being vegetarian in Iceland is entirely manageable. You'll just need to:

  • Be comfortable with a somewhat narrower range of fresh produce than you're used to
  • Accept that specialty ingredients cost more
  • Learn to love root vegetables, greenhouse produce, and Icelandic dairy
  • Consider ordering some specialty items online (though customs duties apply to food imports)
  • Embrace Icelandic skyr — it's legitimately one of the best foods in the country and it's vegetarian

Bottom line: Being vegetarian in Reykjavík in 2026 is absolutely doable. It's not convenient in the way Dublin or London is, but you won't go hungry, and the options are genuinely improving year on year. Outside Reykjavík, plan ahead.

4. Language & Integration

Icelandic: Let's Be Honest

Icelandic is hard. It's a highly inflected Old Norse language with four cases, three genders, and complex verb conjugations. Most language resources rank it among the hardest European languages for English speakers.

  • Do you need it? For daily survival, no. Most Icelanders under 60 speak excellent English, and you can handle shops, restaurants, and basic services in English.
  • For career advancement? Absolutely yes. Without Icelandic, your career ceiling is low and your job options are narrow. This is especially true for Pin1's DEI/equality ambitions (policy work, government-adjacent roles) and for Pin2's broader marketing ambitions beyond the tourism/tech niche.
  • For social integration? This is the critical one. Icelanders are friendly but not naturally inclusive of outsiders. Social groups are formed in childhood and are remarkably stable. Without Icelandic, you'll struggle to break into genuine Icelandic social circles. You'll likely end up primarily socialising with other expats.

Integration Reality

  • Icelandic classes are available through Mímir, Tin Can Factory, and the Multicultural Centre. Some are government-subsidised.
  • Budget 1–2 years of serious study to reach functional conversational Icelandic. Professional fluency takes 3–5 years for most people.
  • The government has been improving integration support, but it's still a work in progress. Don't expect the hand-holding some Scandinavian countries provide.

5. Weather & Quality of Life

Weather — The Unvarnished Truth

  • Winter (Nov–Feb): 4–5 hours of usable daylight. In December, the sun rises around 11:15 and sets around 15:30, but it barely clears the horizon — it's more like extended twilight. It's dark when you go to work and dark when you come home. Temperatures hover around -5°C to +3°C. Wind is the real enemy — not the cold, but the relentless, bone-cutting wind. Storms can be genuinely frightening.
  • Summer (Jun–Aug): The payoff. Near-24-hour daylight. Temperatures of 10–18°C (yes, that's summer). The landscape transforms. This is when Iceland earns its keep.
  • Spring/Autumn: Unpredictable. You can experience all four seasons in a single day. This is not a joke.

Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD)

This is real and widespread. Many Icelanders take Vitamin D supplements, use light therapy lamps, and still struggle. If either of you is prone to depression or anxiety, take the winter darkness extremely seriously. It has broken many newcomers.

Quality of Life — The Good

  • Safety: Iceland is absurdly safe. Virtually no violent crime. You can walk anywhere in Reykjavík at any hour.
  • Nature: Extraordinary. Glaciers, volcanoes, hot springs, waterfalls — all within a few hours' drive. The geothermal swimming pools (sundlaugar) are a cornerstone of Icelandic life and genuine joy.
  • LGBTQ+ rights: Iceland is one of the best countries in the world for LGBTQ+ people. Jóhanna Sigurðardóttir was the world's first openly gay head of government (2009–2013). Same-sex marriage legal since 2010. Reykjavík Pride is massive relative to the population. Samtökin '78 is active and welcoming. You will face essentially zero discrimination in daily life.
  • Work-life balance: Generally good. Standard work week is 36–40 hours following recent union negotiations. Generous parental leave (though not relevant now, good to know).
  • Air quality: Pristine. Geothermal heating means almost no air pollution.

Quality of Life — The Challenging

  • Isolation: Iceland is an island in the North Atlantic. Flights to Europe take 3+ hours and aren't cheap (€200–600 return to Ireland depending on season). You will feel far from family and friends.
  • Small-town dynamics: Reykjavík metro has ~230,000 people. Everyone knows everyone. This can be cosy or claustrophobic depending on your temperament.
  • Limited consumer choice: Want a specific product? It might not exist here. Online shopping from abroad means customs duties and long waits. This is especially relevant for vegetarians looking for specialty ingredients or products.

6. Healthcare & Social Services

The System

Iceland has a universal public healthcare system funded through taxes. Once you have a kennitala (national ID) and are registered as a resident, you're in the system.

How It Works

  • You register with a primary care health centre (heilsugæsla). GP visits cost ~ISK 2,500–4,000 (€16–€26) per visit — not free, but subsidised.
  • Specialist referrals go through your GP. Wait times for non-urgent specialists can be weeks to months. This is a real pain point.
  • Hospital care (Landspítalinn is the main hospital) is heavily subsidised. You pay a fraction of the cost.
  • Dental care is largely private and expensive. A basic check-up and cleaning runs €150–250.
  • Mental health services: Available but strained. Wait times for a psychologist through the public system can be 3–6 months. Private psychologists charge ~ISK 18,000–25,000 (€120–€165) per session. Given the winter darkness and the stress of relocation, having a mental health plan is not optional — it's essential.
  • Prescriptions have a co-pay system that caps your annual out-of-pocket medication costs.

For New Residents

  • You become eligible for the public healthcare system once you've been registered with a legal domicile in Iceland for 6 months (for EEA citizens). During this waiting period, your European Health Insurance Card (EHIC) can cover emergency care, but it's wise to have private health insurance for the gap.
  • Register at Þjóðskrá (Registers Iceland) as soon as you arrive.

7. Pet Import — Bringing Your Cats

This is the section that breaks people. Read carefully.

Iceland has the strictest pet import regulations in Europe, possibly the world. The island has been isolated from many animal diseases, and the authorities intend to keep it that way.

Requirements

  1. Permit application: You must apply to MAST (Matvælastofnun — the Icelandic Food and Veterinary Authority) for an import permit well in advance. Processing takes several weeks.
  1. Vaccinations & treatments required BEFORE entry:

- Rabies vaccination (must be given after microchipping, at least 60 days before import but not more than 12 months)

- Treatments against specific diseases including:

- Feline distemper (panleukopenia)

- Feline viral rhinotracheitis

- Feline calicivirus

- Feline leukemia (FeLV)

- Treatments against internal parasites (tapeworm, roundworm) and external parasites (fleas, ticks) — specific timing requirements apply

- Blood tests may be required to confirm antibody levels

  1. Veterinary health certificate issued within 10 days of travel
  1. MANDATORY QUARANTINE: 4 weeks minimum (previously was longer, reduced in recent years but still substantial)

- Quarantine is served at the Keldur quarantine facility near Reykjavík

- You cannot visit your cats freely during quarantine — visits are restricted

- Cost: approximately ISK 100,000–180,000 per cat (€660–€1,200 per cat) for the quarantine period, covering housing, feeding, and veterinary supervision

- Total cost for two cats including flights, documentation, and quarantine: budget €2,500–€4,000

  1. Only specific entry points are allowed — typically Keflavík Airport

Timeline

Start this process at least 4–6 months before your planned move. Missing any step means your cats could be refused entry or face extended quarantine.

Emotional Reality

The quarantine is hard on cats and hard on owners. Your cats will be in an unfamiliar facility without you for a month. Some cats handle it fine; others are stressed. There's no way around it — this is the law.

Contact MAST directly for the most current requirements: mast@mast.is. Rules have been updated periodically, so verify everything with them.

8. Community & Social Life

The Reykjavík Social Scene

Reykjavík punches well above its weight culturally. For a city of ~130,000 (metro ~230,000), there's a remarkable amount going on:

  • Music scene: Legendary. Iceland produces an absurd amount of music per capita. Live shows every weekend.
  • Arts & culture: Harpa concert hall, numerous galleries, a thriving literary culture (Icelanders publish more books per capita than anywhere on earth).
  • Nightlife: Concentrated on Laugavegur and surrounding streets. Goes very late on weekends (2–5 AM). The "rúntur" (pub crawl circuit) is a cultural institution.
  • Swimming pools: This cannot be overstated. The sundlaug is where Icelanders actually socialise. Go to the hot pot, sit in the warm water, and talk. This is the closest thing to a social shortcut Iceland offers.
  • LGBTQ+ community: Small but visible and welcoming. Samtökin '78 runs events and a community centre. Kiki Queer Bar on Laugavegur is the main LGBTQ+ venue. Pride in August is enormous.
  • Vegetarian/vegan community: There is a small but growing community around plant-based living. Facebook groups and occasional meetups exist. It's niche, but it's another way to connect with like-minded people.

Making Friends — The Hard Part

  • Icelanders are friendly but not easy to befriend deeply. Social circles are established in childhood (often literally from age 6 in school) and tend to be quite closed.
  • The expat community is your most likely initial social network. There are Facebook groups, meetups, and organisations for foreign residents.
  • Joining activities is the best path in: sports clubs, choir, volunteer work, hobby groups. Icelanders open up through shared activities, not through casual socialising.
  • It takes 2–3 years for most foreigners to feel they have genuine Icelandic friends. Some never fully crack it. This is the most common complaint from long-term foreign residents.

9. Practical Challenges for Newcomers

Bureaucracy

  • Kennitala: Your national ID number. You need this for everything — bank accounts, phone contracts, rental agreements, healthcare. Getting it requires registering your domicile. This is your first priority upon arrival.
  • Banking: Opening a bank account requires a kennitala and proof of address. Can be frustratingly circular (need address to get bank, need bank to pay rent for address).
  • Driving licence: Irish licences are valid, but you should exchange it for an Icelandic one within 6 months of becoming a resident.

Housing Shortage (Again, Because It's That Important)

I cannot stress this enough. The housing shortage in Reykjavík is acute. Start looking before you move. Be prepared to accept something imperfect initially. Having a local contact who can vouch for you or help with the search is enormously valuable. Finding a place that accepts two cats narrows the field even further.

Cost Shock

Even coming from Dublin, which is expensive, the cumulative cost of everything in Iceland will wear on you. It's not just rent — it's the €14 pint, the €20 pizza, the €80 weekly grocery shop for two. It adds up relentlessly. As vegetarians, you'll save a bit by not buying Icelandic meat and fish (which are pricey), but you'll pay a premium on imported produce and specialty plant-based products. It roughly evens out.

Loneliness & Isolation

This is the silent killer of relocations to Iceland. The combination of:

  • Dark winters
  • Small social circle
  • Language barrier
  • Physical distance from family/friends
  • Expensive flights home

...creates a loneliness that many newcomers underestimate. The first winter is the hardest. If you survive the first full winter and still want to stay, you'll probably make it.

Practical Tips

  • Join a swimming pool immediately. Get a multi-pool pass (Reykjavík city card or similar). Go regularly.
  • Learn to drive if you don't already. Outside Reykjavík, a car is not optional. Even within the city, winter weather makes public transport unreliable.
  • Build an emergency fund of at least 3–6 months' expenses before moving.
  • Get your cat paperwork started the moment you decide to move.
  • Connect with the expat community before arriving — Facebook groups like "Americans in Iceland," "Foreigners in Iceland," and similar Irish/British groups exist and are helpful.
  • Stock up at the duty-free shop on arrival at Keflavík — alcohol and some food items are dramatically cheaper there.
  • Find your Bónus and Krónan — these are the budget supermarkets. Hagkaup is the one with the best vegetarian/specialty range but costs more. Shop strategically.

Final Honest Assessment

Should you move to Iceland? It depends on what you're optimising for.

Iceland is excellent if you value:

  • Safety and social stability
  • LGBTQ+ acceptance (world-class)
  • Nature and outdoor lifestyle
  • Clean air, clean water, geothermal everything
  • A small, tight-knit community (once you're in)
  • Work-life balance
  • Gender equality (strong institutional framework — relevant for Pin1's DEI interests)

Iceland is difficult if you value:

  • Career advancement in the short term (especially in niche fields like DEI)
  • Affordable living
  • Warm weather and sunlight
  • Easy social integration
  • Proximity to family
  • Consumer variety and convenience (including vegetarian specialty products)
  • A large, diverse job market

Specific to your profiles:

  • Pin1: Iceland's equality framework is genuinely world-leading, but the career infrastructure for DEI as a standalone profession isn't there yet. You'd be a pioneer, which is either exciting or exhausting depending on your disposition. HR + AI + inclusion expertise is a strong combination — just don't expect "DEI Manager" to appear on Icelandic job boards.
  • Pin2: The marketing/comms landscape is small but workable, especially if you target tourism, tech, or go freelance. Human Geography as a study path at the University of Iceland is genuinely compelling — Iceland is a living laboratory for the discipline. The near-free tuition for EEA citizens makes this very accessible.
  • Vegetarian life: Manageable in Reykjavík, requires planning outside it. Not a dealbreaker, but add it to the list of small daily frictions that accumulate.
  • Cats: Start the MAST process immediately if you're serious. This is the longest lead-time item in your entire move.

My honest recommendation: Come for a winter visit first — not summer, not autumn, winter. Spend at least two weeks here in January or February. Experience the darkness, the wind, the prices, the isolation. Eat at restaurants and try to find vegetarian options outside the city centre. If you still want to come after that, you'll come with open eyes.
