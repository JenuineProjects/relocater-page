Full research report — Relocater Helper for Pin1 & Pin2

🇳🇱 The Netherlands as a Relocation Destination — An Honest Assessment

By Annelies — born, raised, and still living here

Look, the Netherlands is a genuinely good country to live in. But I'm not going to sell it to you like a tourism board. You're making a life decision, so here's the real picture.

1. Job Market & Career Prospects

Pin1 — HR / DEI + AI for Business

This is actually a very strong profile for the Netherlands right now. Let me break it down into the two angles:

DEI (Diversity, Equity & Inclusion):

The Netherlands has one of the more developed DEI landscapes in continental Europe, and it's growing. Here's why:

  • Multinationals headquartered here — Shell, Unilever, Philips, ASML, Booking.com, Adyen, ING, ABN AMRO, Heineken — nearly all have dedicated DEI teams or at minimum a DEI lead. These roles are almost always conducted in English.
  • The EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) and increasing ESG requirements have pushed Dutch companies to formalize their DEI strategies. This means more roles are being created, not fewer.
  • Consultancies with DEI practices — firms like Deloitte, PwC, EY, McKinsey, and specialized boutiques (Include, Diversity at Work / Diversiteit in Bedrijf, Equileap) operate here. There's also a growing ecosystem of smaller DEI consultancies in Amsterdam and The Hague.
  • The Dutch government has its own diversity policies and the Sociaal-Economische Raad (SER) has been pushing for diversity targets on corporate boards. Public sector DEI roles exist too, though these more often require Dutch.
  • Honest caveat: The Dutch DEI conversation is real but sometimes lags behind the Anglo-American one. The Netherlands has its own history with race and diversity (look up the Zwarte Piet debate to see how complex and heated it gets). Some Dutch companies treat DEI as a checkbox rather than a strategic function. You may find yourself educating as much as implementing.
  • Salary range: DEI-specific roles range from €50,000–€75,000 in corporate settings, potentially higher at senior levels in multinationals. In consultancy, it varies widely.

HR + AI for Business:

This combination remains a strong differentiator. Companies are actively looking for people who can bridge HR and AI/data — workforce analytics, AI-driven recruitment tools, HR tech platforms. Amsterdam, Eindhoven (Brainport region), and Utrecht are the strongest markets.

  • HR roles that are English-only exist, but they're concentrated in international companies, scale-ups, and multinationals.
  • Pure HR generalist roles at Dutch companies? You'll need Dutch. Period. Employment law here is specific, works councils (ondernemingsraad) operate in Dutch, and most HR admin is in Dutch.
  • "People Analytics" roles are growing and more likely to be English-friendly.
  • Salary range: €45,000–€65,000 for mid-level HR/People Analytics. Senior can go higher, especially in tech.

The sweet spot for Pin1: A DEI role at a multinational that also values the AI/data angle — using data to measure inclusion, bias in hiring algorithms, diversity metrics dashboards. This intersection is niche, in demand, and pays well. Companies like Booking.com, ING, and Unilever are exactly the type of employers to target.

Pin2 — Marketing, Communications, Digital Content & Human Geography

Let me cover the full range here:

Marketing / Communications / Digital Content Creation:

Opening this up beyond NGOs significantly improves Pin2's prospects. The Dutch marketing and communications sector is robust across multiple industries.

  • Corporate marketing: Multinationals, tech companies, and scale-ups all need content creators, social media managers, digital marketers, and communications professionals. English-language roles are common in international-facing companies.
  • Creative agencies: Amsterdam has a world-class advertising and creative agency scene — 72andSunny, Wieden+Kennedy (they have an Amsterdam office), DDB, TBWA, Dept, MediaMonks (now S4 Capital). Rotterdam's creative scene is also growing fast. These agencies frequently hire international talent and work in English.
  • Tech & startups: The Amsterdam/Utrecht startup ecosystem is thriving and almost entirely English-speaking. Marketing/growth roles are plentiful. Companies like Messagebird (now Bird), Mollie, Bunq, Picnic, and dozens of others.
  • NGO sector: Still relevant — The Hague remains the hub for international organizations (Oxfam Novib, Hivos, Cordaid, ICC ecosystem, UN-adjacent bodies). Competitive and lower-paid, but Pin2's experience here is directly transferable.
  • Public sector communications: Government bodies, municipalities, and semi-public organizations (ZBO's) hire communications professionals, but these roles almost always require Dutch.
  • Freelancing (ZZP): Very common in the Dutch creative/marketing sector. If Pin2 builds a network, freelancing is viable and well-supported structurally (though you need to handle your own taxes and insurance).
  • Salary range: €35,000–€55,000 for mid-level marketing/comms roles. Agencies and tech companies tend to pay more; NGOs less. Senior digital marketing roles in tech can reach €60,000–€70,000+.

Human Geography:

This is interesting. The Netherlands actually has a strong tradition in human geography — it's one of those fields where Dutch universities punch well above their weight.

  • Academic route: The University of Amsterdam (UvA), Utrecht University, and the University of Groningen all have well-regarded human geography programs. If Pin2 wants to pursue a master's or PhD, these are excellent options. Many programs are in English. Tuition for EU citizens is ~€2,500/year (much cheaper than Ireland or the UK).
  • Applied roles: Human geography skills translate into urban planning (ruimtelijke ordening), policy research, GIS analysis, social impact assessment, and consultancy. Organizations like PBL (Netherlands Environmental Assessment Agency), KWR, and various municipal planning departments hire people with this background.
  • Think tanks and research: The Netherlands has institutions like the WRR (Wetenschappelijke Raad voor het Regeringsbeleid), CPB, SCP, and various university-affiliated research centers where human geography expertise is valued.
  • Reality check: Pure human geography career roles outside academia are limited and usually require Dutch. The practical move would be to combine geography knowledge with GIS/data skills or urban planning qualifications.
  • Studying while working: The Dutch system supports part-time study well. Pin2 could work in marketing/comms while pursuing a part-time master's in human geography. This is a very Dutch approach — pragmatic and common.

Best Cities to Target (Ranked)

  1. The Hague — Best balance for both of you. International organizations and NGOs for Pin2, multinationals and government-adjacent DEI roles for Pin1, slightly more affordable than Amsterdam. Strong expat infrastructure.
  2. Amsterdam — Most English-friendly job market, strongest for both DEI roles (multinational HQs) and creative/digital marketing. But housing is a nightmare (more on that below).
  3. Utrecht — Lovely city, growing job market, excellent university (relevant for Pin2's geography interest), slightly more affordable, but fewer NGO and agency roles.
  4. Rotterdam — Underrated, up-and-coming, more affordable, excellent creative/agency scene (good for Pin2), but smaller DEI-specific market for Pin1. Strong urban planning sector links to human geography.
  5. Eindhoven — Strong tech (ASML, Philips), great for Pin1's AI angle, but limited for Pin2's interests and culturally less vibrant.

2. Cost of Living & Housing

The Housing Crisis — Let's Not Dance Around It

This is the single biggest challenge you will face. I cannot overstate this. The Dutch housing market (woningmarkt) is in a severe crisis. It has been for years, and as of 2026, it is still brutal.

Realistic Rents (2026, unfurnished, per month)

| City | Studio/1BR | 2BR Apartment |

|------|-----------|---------------|

| Amsterdam | €1,400–€1,800 | €1,800–€2,500 |

| The Hague | €1,100–€1,500 | €1,400–€1,900 |

| Utrecht | €1,200–€1,600 | €1,500–€2,100 |

| Rotterdam | €1,000–€1,400 | €1,300–€1,800 |

| Eindhoven | €900–€1,300 | €1,200–€1,600 |

Critical things to know:

  • Social housing (sociale huurwoning) has waiting lists of 8–15 years in Amsterdam, 5–8 years elsewhere. This is not an option for you.
  • The private rental market is what you'll use. Expect to pay 1–2 months deposit + 1 month rent in advance.
  • Landlords often require proof of income (typically 3–3.5x the monthly rent as gross salary). With two incomes, this helps.
  • Scams are rampant. Never pay before viewing. Never wire money abroad. Use platforms like Funda, Pararius, or Kamernet. Facebook groups are riddled with scams.
  • "Unfurnished" in the Netherlands often means literally unfurnished — no flooring, no light fixtures, sometimes no kitchen. Yes, really. You may need to install your own floor.
  • With 2 cats, your options narrow further. Not all landlords accept pets, and those who do may charge extra.

Other Cost of Living

  • Groceries: Comparable to Ireland. Albert Heijn is your Tesco. Lidl and Aldi are cheaper.
  • Eating out: Slightly cheaper than Dublin, but the Netherlands is not a cheap-dining culture. A dinner out for two: €50–€80 easily.
  • Transport: Excellent public transport (OV-chipkaart), but not cheap. A monthly subscription (Dal Vrij or Altijd Vrij) runs €100–€350+. But honestly? You'll cycle everywhere. Everyone does.
  • Utilities: €150–€250/month for a 2BR apartment (gas, electricity, water). Dutch homes are increasingly insulated but many older apartments are drafty.

3. Language & Integration

Do You Really Need Dutch?

For work: Depends on the sector. In international companies, tech, DEI roles at multinationals, and some NGOs — no, English is fine day-to-day. In Dutch organizations, government, healthcare, or anything client-facing with Dutch customers — absolutely yes.

For life: Here's the truth nobody puts in the brochure. Almost every Dutch person speaks English. This is simultaneously a blessing and a curse.

  • Blessing: You can survive perfectly well without Dutch for years.
  • Curse: You will never truly integrate without it. Dutch people will switch to English the moment they detect your accent, which makes practising nearly impossible. Social groups, neighborhood WhatsApp chats, birthday parties — all in Dutch. You'll be the person smiling and nodding while understanding 30% of the conversation.

The Bureaucracy Problem

Government letters (brieven van de gemeente), tax correspondence (Belastingdienst), health insurance documents, rental contracts — all in Dutch. Google Translate will be your best friend, but it's not perfect for legal/bureaucratic Dutch, which is its own dialect of suffering.

Dutch Directness

We are direct. Blunt. We consider it honest; others consider it rude. Your manager will tell you your presentation was "not good" without softening it. Your colleague will tell you your idea won't work, to your face, in a meeting. Coming from Ireland, where communication tends to be more indirect, this can feel shocking. It's not personal. It's just... Dutch.

Integration Advice

  • Take Dutch classes. Even A2/B1 level makes a massive difference socially.
  • The inburgering (civic integration) requirement likely won't apply to you as EU/EEA citizens coming from Ireland, but learning Dutch is still essential for real integration.

4. Vegetarian Life in the Netherlands

The Good News: You Picked Well

The Netherlands is genuinely one of the most vegetarian- and vegan-friendly countries in Europe. This wasn't always the case — traditional Dutch food is heavy on meat and dairy (stamppot met rookworst, anyone?) — but the shift over the past decade has been dramatic. You will eat well here.

Supermarkets

Albert Heijn (AH): The dominant supermarket chain. Their vegetarian and vegan range is extensive and keeps growing. They carry their own plant-based line (AH Terra) covering everything from plant-based mince and burgers to dairy-free yoghurt and cheese. You'll also find major brands like The Vegetarian Butcher (a Dutch company, now owned by Unilever — they're everywhere), Vivera, Garden Gourmet, and Beyond Meat. AH has clearly labelled vegetarian and vegan shelf sections in most stores. The quality is genuinely good.

Jumbo: The second-largest chain. Similar range to AH, slightly cheaper on some items. Their own plant-based line is solid. Good selection of tofu, tempeh (the Netherlands has a colonial history with Indonesia, so tempeh has been available here far longer than in most European countries), and meat substitutes.

Lidl & Aldi: Budget supermarkets. Both have expanded their vegetarian/vegan ranges significantly. Lidl's Vemondo line is surprisingly good for the price. Aldi's My Best Veggie range covers the basics. Not as extensive as AH or Jumbo, but perfectly workable for daily cooking.

Ekoplaza: This is your organic/health food chain. If you care about organic produce, sustainable sourcing, or want specialty items (nutritional yeast, specific plant milks, organic tempeh, specialty grains), Ekoplaza is the place. It's more expensive, but the quality is excellent. They have a very strong vegetarian and vegan selection, and the staff generally know their products well. Think of it as the Dutch equivalent of a well-stocked organic shop.

Turkish and Asian supermarkets (toko's): Don't overlook these. They're everywhere in Dutch cities and are goldmines for vegetarian cooking — cheap fresh vegetables, tofu, every spice you need, legumes in bulk, Indonesian and Surinamese ingredients. A toko in the city center will have better and cheaper produce for vegetarian cooking than most mainstream supermarkets.

Eating Out

The restaurant scene for vegetarians is strong, especially in the bigger cities:

  • Amsterdam: You're spoiled for choice. Dedicated vegetarian/vegan restaurants like Mr. & Mrs. Watson (vegan fine dining), Meatless District, TerraZen Centre, The Golden Temple, Vegan Junk Food Bar (multiple locations). Plus, virtually every restaurant in Amsterdam has substantial vegetarian options now — it's expected.
  • The Hague: Smaller scene but still good. Hagedis, Monsoon, 42 Degrees Raw — and most mainstream restaurants have solid veggie options.
  • Rotterdam: Great vegan/veggie scene — Spirit, Gare du Nord, Bertmans. Rotterdam's food culture is more experimental and diverse than Amsterdam's in some ways.
  • Utrecht: Oproer, Broers, Gys — charming veggie-friendly spots. Smaller city, smaller scene, but enough to keep you happy.

Day-to-day ease:

  • Most Dutch restaurants have at least 2–3 vegetarian mains. Vegan options are increasingly standard. It's rare to walk into a restaurant and find nothing — though traditional Dutch brown cafés (bruine kroegen) serving bitterballen and croquettes may have limited options.
  • Surinamese restaurants are a hidden gem for vegetarians. Surinamese cuisine is widely available in the Netherlands (historical colonial ties), and it includes excellent vegetarian dishes — roti with potato and egg, vegetables in masala, bara (lentil fritters). Cheap and delicious.
  • Indonesian restaurants (rijsttafel) often have vegetarian versions of the multi-dish rice table. Tempeh, tofu, sayur lodeh, gado-gado — all naturally plant-based or easily adapted.
  • Falafel and Middle Eastern food is everywhere and naturally vegetarian-friendly.

Delivery apps: Thuisbezorgd (Takeaway.com — it's actually a Dutch company), Uber Eats, and Deliveroo all have vegetarian filter options. Plenty of choice in any city.

Cost

  • Vegetarian cooking at home is affordable. Legumes, vegetables, rice, pasta, tofu, and tempeh are cheap. Meat substitutes are slightly more expensive than meat equivalent (a pack of Vegetarian Butcher "chicken" pieces is ~€3–4 vs ~€2.50–3 for actual chicken), but the gap is closing.
  • Eating out vegetarian is no more expensive than eating out with meat — unlike some countries where vegetarian options feel like an afterthought, Dutch restaurants price veggie mains comparably.
  • Ekoplaza and specialty items will cost more. Budget your organic shop visits if money is tight.

The Overall Verdict on Vegetarian Life

Coming from Ireland, you will find the Netherlands significantly easier for vegetarian living. More variety in supermarkets, more restaurant options, more cultural normalcy around plant-based eating. The Dutch Voedingscentrum (Nutrition Centre) actively promotes reducing meat consumption, and "flexitarian" eating is mainstream. Nobody will look at you strangely for being vegetarian. It's just... normal here.

5. Weather & Quality of Life

Weather — Brace Yourselves

If you think Irish weather is grey, well... you'll feel right at home. The Netherlands has:

  • 200+ overcast days per year. Grey. Flat grey sky meeting flat grey land at the horizon.
  • Wind. Constant. The country is flat and near the sea. Cycling into a headwind with horizontal rain is a character-building Dutch experience.
  • Winters: Not extremely cold (0–5°C typically), but dark. Sunrise at 8:45, sunset at 16:30 in December. SAD (Seasonal Affective Depression) is genuinely common.
  • Summers: Can be lovely (20–28°C), but increasingly we get heatwaves (35°C+) and most Dutch homes have no air conditioning.
  • Coming from Ireland: Honestly, the weather is comparable. Slightly colder winters, slightly warmer summers, roughly the same amount of grey misery.

Cycling Culture

This is genuinely wonderful. The cycling infrastructure is world-class. Dedicated bike lanes (fietspaden) everywhere, bike parking garages, traffic lights for cyclists. You will cycle to work, to the supermarket, to the pub, in the rain, in a suit, carrying groceries. It becomes your default mode of transport and it's actually one of the best things about living here.

Buy a sturdy Dutch city bike (omafiets). Don't buy an expensive one — bike theft is a national sport. Budget €100–€200 for a decent second-hand one, and always use two locks.

Work-Life Balance

Generally good. The Netherlands consistently ranks high for work-life balance globally. Part-time work is normalized (we're sometimes called the "part-time economy"). Many people work 32 or 36 hours/week. Fridays off is common. Parental leave is decent.

Standard vacation: 20 days minimum by law + public holidays. Many companies offer 25.

6. Healthcare & Social Services

How It Works

The Dutch healthcare system is insurance-based, not state-funded like Ireland's (well, Ireland's is a hybrid mess anyway).

  • Zorgverzekering (health insurance) is mandatory for every resident. You must arrange it within 4 months of registering.
  • Basic package (basispakket): ~€130–€170/month in 2026. Covers GP visits, hospital care, mental healthcare (with referral), maternity, and more.
  • Eigen risico (excess/deductible): Currently ~€385/year. You pay this before insurance kicks in for most specialist care. GP visits are exempt from the excess.
  • Dental: NOT covered in basic insurance for adults. You need aanvullende verzekering (supplementary insurance) for dental, or pay out of pocket. Dental care is expensive here.

The GP (Huisarts) System

  • You must register with a local GP (huisarts). They are the gatekeeper to all specialist care.
  • Finding a GP accepting new patients is difficult, especially in Amsterdam and Utrecht. Start looking immediately when you arrive. Some areas have waiting lists.
  • The Dutch GP philosophy is... minimalist. If you come from a culture where the doctor prescribes antibiotics for a cold, prepare to be told to take a paracetamol and wait it out. "Paracetamolletje en afwachten" is practically the national medical motto. This frustrates many expats.

Mental Healthcare

  • Covered under basic insurance with a GP referral, but waiting times are long — 3–6 months or more for non-urgent psychological care. This is a known national problem.
  • Private psychologists/therapists exist but cost €80–€150/session out of pocket.

LGBTQ+ Healthcare

The Netherlands was the first country in the world to legalize same-sex marriage (2001). LGBTQ+ healthcare is generally good. Amsterdam is particularly strong. No issues with being an LGBTQ+ couple in healthcare settings.

7. Pet Import (Cats)

EU Pet Passport Rules (Ireland → Netherlands)

Since Ireland is an EU member, this is relatively straightforward:

  1. Microchip: Both cats must be microchipped (ISO 11784/11785 standard). This is almost certainly already done if they're registered in Ireland.
  2. Rabies vaccination: Must be administered after microchipping. Must be at least 21 days before travel.
  3. EU Pet Passport: Obtain this from your Irish vet. It records the microchip number, rabies vaccination, and owner details.
  4. Tapeworm treatment: Ireland has specific rules for leaving, and the Netherlands doesn't require tapeworm treatment for cats arriving from Ireland, but check the latest NVWA (Dutch food and consumer product safety authority) guidance closer to your travel date.
  5. Maximum 5 pets per person when travelling (you have 2, so fine).

At the Border

  • If flying: Airlines have their own pet policies. KLM, for instance, allows small cats in-cabin in approved carriers. Check weight limits.
  • If driving/ferry: Ferry companies (Stena Line, Irish Ferries) have pet policies — some allow pets in cabins, others require them to stay in the car or designated areas.
  • At Dutch arrival: Customs may ask to see the EU Pet Passport. In practice, within the EU, checks are rare, but have the documentation ready.

Once in the Netherlands

  • Register with a local vet (dierenarts).
  • Annual vaccinations and check-ups are your responsibility and not covered by any "pet insurance" you might buy (pet insurance exists here — huisdierenverzekering — and can be useful for unexpected vet bills).
  • Cats are commonly kept indoors in Dutch apartments, especially in cities. Outdoor cats in urban areas are less common than in Ireland.

8. Community & Social Life

LGBTQ+ Community

The Netherlands is genuinely one of the best places in the world for LGBTQ+ people. Amsterdam is the obvious hub — the Reguliersdwarsstraat area, Canal Pride (first weekend of August), extensive queer nightlife and culture. But even in smaller cities, acceptance is high. That said, I'll be honest: we're progressive but not immune to homophobia, particularly in more religious communities (Bible Belt) and some immigrant communities. Day-to-day in the Randstad (Amsterdam/Rotterdam/The Hague/Utrecht)? You'll be absolutely fine.

The Dreaded Dutch Social Circle Problem

This is the single most common complaint from every expat I've ever met. Here's the reality:

  • Dutch people form their core friend group in childhood/university, and then the door closes. We are friendly, but friendship is a slow, structured process. We schedule coffee three weeks in advance. We have "agenda culture" (agendacultuur) — if it's not in the calendar, it doesn't happen.
  • You will make expat friends easily. You will struggle to make Dutch friends.
  • Tips that actually work: Join a sports club (sportvereniging), a borrel (drinks gathering), volunteer, join a choir or hobby group. These structured social settings are how Dutch adults make new friends too.
  • It takes 2–3 years to build a real social circle. Be patient.

Expat Community

Large and active, especially in Amsterdam, The Hague, and Eindhoven. Meetup groups, InterNations events, expat Facebook groups, international clubs. You won't lack for English-speaking social opportunities. The risk is getting stuck in the "expat bubble" and never integrating into Dutch life.

Arts, Culture & Nightlife

Genuinely excellent. World-class museums (Rijksmuseum, Van Gogh, Stedelijk, Mauritshuis), thriving music scene, good theater (mostly in Dutch, some English). Nightlife is strong, especially Amsterdam and Rotterdam. Rotterdam's cultural scene is underrated and arguably more interesting than Amsterdam's at this point.

9. Practical Challenges for Newcomers

The First Month Bureaucratic Marathon

Here's roughly what you'll need to do, and the order matters:

  1. Find housing — You need a registered address to do everything else. This is the bottleneck. Some people use temporary housing/Airbnb, but you need a real address to register.
  2. Register at the municipality (gemeente) — Make an appointment (weeks in advance in Amsterdam) to register at the Bevolkingsregister. You'll get your BSN (Burgerservicenummer — citizen service number). This is your key to everything.
  3. Open a bank account — You need a BSN for this. ING, ABN AMRO, Rabobank, or Bunq. Dutch banking runs on iDEAL payments; credit cards are rarely used for daily purchases.
  4. Get health insurance — Within 4 months of registration. Compare at Independer.nl or Zorgwijzer.nl.
  5. Apply for DigiD — Your digital identity for all government services, tax filing, health insurance, etc. Takes 1–2 weeks to arrive by post.
  6. Register with a GP — Start immediately, as practices are full.

The 30% Tax Ruling (30%-regeling)

This is a significant financial benefit if you qualify:

  • What it is: 30% of your gross salary is paid tax-free, as a compensation for the extra costs of living abroad. This effectively boosts your net salary significantly.
  • Eligibility (2026 rules — check for updates): You must be recruited from abroad (or within 150 km of the Dutch border — Ireland qualifies), have specific expertise not readily available in the Netherlands, and meet a minimum salary threshold (~€46,000 gross in 2026, lower for under-30s with a master's degree — ~€35,000).
  • Duration: Currently 5 years, but the government has been reducing this. The 2024 changes capped the tax-free component at 27% after 20 months. Check the latest rules — this keeps changing.
  • Pin1 is more likely to qualify than Pin2, given the salary thresholds and "specific expertise" requirement. AI for Business with an MSc is a strong case. A DEI role at a multinational could also qualify if the salary threshold is met and the employer supports the application.
  • Pin2 may qualify if landing a role above the salary threshold, particularly if the employer can argue specific expertise. Digital marketing/content roles at international companies sometimes meet the bar.
  • Apply through your employer. They must submit the application.

Things That Will Frustrate You

  • Everything closes early. Shops close at 18:00 on weekdays (some until 21:00 on koopavond, usually Thursday). Sundays many shops open 12:00–17:00. Coming from Ireland, this may or may not be different.
  • Dutch bureaucracy is digitized but rigid. The systems work well, but if your situation doesn't fit the standard template, good luck.
  • Birthday culture. You will sit in a circle. You will congratulate everyone in the room on the birthday person's birthday. You will eat a piece of cake on a small plate. You will drink one cup of coffee. This is the tradition and you will participate. (Gefeliciteerd!)
  • Noise rules. Your neighbors will complain if you're loud after 22:00. Apartment living requires adjusting to Dutch noise etiquette.
  • Tipping. Not expected but appreciated. Round up or add 5–10%. Servers earn a real wage here.

Final Verdict

Should you move to the Netherlands?

For your specific situation, the Netherlands is a strong option, and the updated picture of your profiles makes it even stronger than before.

Genuine strengths:

  • Excellent LGBTQ+ rights and acceptance
  • Strong and growing DEI job market for Pin1, especially at multinationals
  • Broad marketing/comms/digital sector for Pin2, with options across corporate, agency, NGO, and tech
  • Human geography is a respected academic field here — realistic path for Pin2 to study part-time
  • One of Europe's best countries for vegetarian living — you will not struggle to eat well
  • World-class infrastructure, cycling, and public transport
  • Good work-life balance
  • EU freedom of movement from Ireland makes logistics simpler
  • Cat import is straightforward within the EU

Real concerns:

  • Housing will be your biggest obstacle. Start looking months before you plan to move. Budget aggressively.
  • Social integration is slow and can be lonely in the first 1–2 years
  • Learning Dutch is technically optional but practically essential for long-term happiness
  • Healthcare gatekeeping frustrates many newcomers
  • The DEI field in the Netherlands, while growing, can sometimes feel less mature than in the UK or Ireland — be prepared to be a builder, not just an implementer

My honest recommendation: Target The Hague or Amsterdam as your primary cities. The Hague gives you the best balance — international organizations, multinationals nearby, more affordable housing, strong expat infrastructure. Amsterdam gives you the biggest job market and the most vibrant vegetarian/LGBTQ+ scene, but at a painful housing premium. Utrecht is a strong third option, especially if Pin2 wants to study human geography at the university there.

Start applying for jobs before you move. Having a job offer makes everything easier — housing applications, 30% ruling, and peace of mind. For Pin1, target DEI or People Analytics roles at multinationals directly. For Pin2, cast a wide net across marketing/comms roles in tech, agencies, and international organizations simultaneously.

Veel succes. (Good luck.) You'll need some, but honestly? Your profiles are well-suited to what the Netherlands needs right now. The housing hunt will be the hardest part. Everything else is manageable.
