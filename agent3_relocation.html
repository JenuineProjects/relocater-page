What This Means Practically

As Irish citizens, you have it easy legally in both countries. No visa applications, no work permits, no sponsor requirements. You show up, register, get your number, and you're in the system. The difference is:

  • Iceland (EEA): Your rights derive from the EEA Agreement, not EU membership. Functionally identical for residency and work, but Iceland is outside the EU customs union, VAT area, and many EU regulations. This matters for things like pet import (no EU Pet Passport system) and consumer goods (customs duties on online orders).
  • Netherlands (EU): Full EU member. Everything is smoother for intra-EU moves — pets, belongings, professional qualifications, healthcare portability.

Vegetarian-Specific Financial Considerations

This is worth breaking out separately because your diet affects your monthly budget in meaningfully different ways depending on the country:

Iceland:

  • Vegetarian grocery costs are a mixed bag. You save by not buying Icelandic meat and fish (which are genuinely expensive). But you spend more on imported produce, specialty plant-based products (tofu, plant milks, meat substitutes), and variety.
  • Björn's estimate: vegetarian groceries for two run €460–€720/month (ISK 70,000–110,000). This is roughly comparable to a meat-inclusive diet — the savings on meat are offset by premiums on plant-based products.
  • Specialty items (tofu, plant-based burgers, oat milk, tempeh) cost 30–50% more than in Ireland. Hagkaup has the best range; Bónus and Krónan are cheaper but more limited.
  • Eating out vegetarian is actually slightly cheaper than average — you're not paying for lamb or fish mains. Budget €12–20 for a casual vegetarian meal, €18–30 at a nicer restaurant.
  • Outside Reykjavík, vegetarian options drop dramatically. Pack food for road trips. Rural gas stations and small-town restaurants may offer little beyond chips and bread.
  • Pro tip: Icelandic greenhouse produce (tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, herbs) is fresh, local, and available year-round thanks to geothermal energy. Learn to love it. Look for "Íslenski garðurinn" branding.

Netherlands:

  • This is where vegetarian life becomes genuinely easier and cheaper than in Iceland. The Netherlands is one of the most vegetarian-friendly countries in Europe.
  • Vegetarian cooking at home is affordable. Legumes, vegetables, rice, pasta, tofu, and tempeh are cheap. The Netherlands' colonial history with Indonesia means tempeh has been available here far longer than in most European countries — it's a staple, not a specialty item.
  • Albert Heijn's AH Terra plant-based line is extensive and well-priced. The Vegetarian Butcher (Dutch company, now Unilever-owned) is everywhere. Lidl's Vemondo range is surprisingly good for budget shopping.
  • Turkish and Asian supermarkets (toko's) are goldmines: cheap fresh vegetables, tofu, bulk legumes, every spice you need, Indonesian and Surinamese ingredients. These will likely become your favourite shops.
  • Ekoplaza is the organic/specialty chain for items like nutritional yeast, specialty plant milks, organic tempeh. More expensive but excellent quality.
  • Eating out vegetarian costs the same as eating out with meat — no premium, no penalty. Surinamese, Indonesian, and Middle Eastern restaurants are particularly good value for vegetarians.
  • Monthly grocery estimate for two vegetarian people: €400–€600 — slightly below average for the Netherlands.

5. Tax Implications

Leaving the Irish Tax System

This applies regardless of which country you choose:

  1. Notify Revenue: File Form P85 (Notification of cessation of employment and departure from the State) via revenue.ie > myAccount. If self-employed, different forms apply.
  2. Tax residency: You remain Irish tax resident for the year of departure if you've been present in Ireland for 183+ days that year. You may be "split-year" resident — Irish resident for the part of the year you were in Ireland.
  3. Ordinarily resident: Even after leaving, you remain "ordinarily resident" for Irish tax purposes for 3 full tax years after departure. This means worldwide income may still be relevant to Irish Revenue, though the double taxation treaties typically prevent actual double taxation.
  4. Irish assets: If you retain Irish rental income, investments, or other Irish-source income, you'll still file Irish tax returns on that income.
  5. PRSI/social insurance: Obtain a U1 form (formerly E301) from the Department of Social Protection — this certifies your social insurance record and is useful for social security purposes in your new country.

What it does: Up to 30% of your gross salary is treated as a tax-free reimbursement for "extraterritorial costs." This can save you thousands per year.

Recommended Setup (Both Countries)

  1. Get a Wise multi-currency account before you leave Ireland — wise.com. Free to open. Holds EUR, ISK, GBP, etc. Excellent exchange rates. Use this to bridge the gap before your local bank account is set up.

8. Key Risks & Pitfalls

Iceland — Most Common Mistakes

  1. Underestimating the winter darkness. This is the #1 reason people leave. If either of you has any history of depression or anxiety, this is not a minor consideration — it's a potential dealbreaker. Sunrise at ~11:15, sunset at ~15:30 in December, and it barely clears the horizon. Visit in January first.
  2. Moving without sufficient savings. The language barrier means employment may take 3–6 months. Running out of money forces a panicked return. Have €25K+ saved.
  3. Starting cat paperwork too late. If you miss a vaccination window or a blood test deadline, your entire timeline shifts by months. Start the MAST process the day you decide.
  4. Expecting to "get by" without Icelandic long-term. You can survive without it, but you can't thrive. Your career stalls, social life stays shallow, and you'll feel like a permanent tourist. This is especially true for Pin1's DEI/equality ambitions — policy work, government-adjacent roles, and union negotiations all happen in Icelandic.

  7. Getting stuck in the expat bubble. The Netherlands makes it so easy to live in English that you can go years without truly integrating. Join Dutch-language activities, a sports club (sportvereniging), or volunteer — these are how Dutch adults make friends too.
